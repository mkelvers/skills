# Landing pages

A landing page is not a homepage. A homepage serves several intents; a landing page wins one: one offer, one audience, one primary action. This file covers strategy, structure and copy. How it should look comes from [visual.md](visual.md).

## Intake

Gather these before designing or writing, asking only for what is missing and in one batch:

- **Purpose:** the one primary action (trial, demo, purchase, waitlist, download), the exact offer, and what counts as a conversion.
- **Audience:** who it's for, the problem they're solving, their top objections, where the traffic comes from, and what they already know when they arrive.
- **Proof:** logos, testimonials, numbers, case studies, screenshots, video, guarantees.
- **Constraints:** brand voice, reference sites they like, mobile priority.

If something can't be answered, assume sensibly, state the assumption in one line, and continue.

## Structure

The usual order, adjusted to what this offer needs:

1. **Hero:** a headline naming the outcome and the audience, a subheadline that adds how, the primary call to action, one proof signal, and a product visual.
2. **The argument:** problem to solution, three to five benefits written as outcomes, how it works in three steps, then social proof.
3. **Objections:** an FAQ of six to twelve questions, risk reversal (trial, cancel anytime, guarantee), and a final call to action identical to the first.

Pick a layout and say why: a classic hero plus sections when a screenshot explains the product; a long-form story when visitors need educating; a minimal page for high-intent traffic or a simple download; a comparison page when people search for alternatives.

## Conversion rules

- **Match the message to the source.** Visitors from an ad should see the ad's promise and tone in the hero.
- **One primary call to action.** Never competing ones above the fold.
- **Benefits before features.** Features say what it does; benefits say what that means for the visitor.
- **Be specific.** "Cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes", not "save time and streamline".
- **Reduce risk** with at least one of: free trial, free plan, no card required, cancel anytime, money-back guarantee.
- **Put proof beside the claim it supports,** and move the FAQ earlier for high-friction offers.

## Copy

- Headlines that work: "{Outcome} without {pain}", "The {category} for {audience}", "{Result} in {time}".
- A subheadline of one or two sentences saying what it is and who it's for.
- Calls to action as a verb plus what they get: "Start free trial", "Book a demo", "Get the checklist".
- No filler: "revolutionise", "supercharge", "cutting-edge", "effortless", "take it to the next level", "world-class". Say what the product does.
- Never invent prices, statistics, customer counts, testimonials or logos for a real product. Use a marked placeholder such as `{{price}}` until the user supplies the fact.

## Build order

Build and review one section at a time, in this order: hero, benefits, how it works, proof, FAQ, final call to action. Screenshot each before moving on. Rebuilding the whole page on every pass loses control of what changed.

## Before launch

- Title, meta description, social image and favicon.
- Privacy and terms links, a working 404, and validation on every form.
- `noindex` on ad-only or short-lived campaign pages; for evergreen pages, the FAQ in plain question-and-answer form, which search and answer engines read.
- No dead links; a link to `#` is either connected or visibly disabled.
- Checked at 390 px wide, with the primary action on the first screen.
